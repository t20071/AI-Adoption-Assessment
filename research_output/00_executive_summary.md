# Executive Summary: Building an AI Adoption Score

## The 10 Most Important Findings

### 1. AI productivity gains are real but bounded
At the task level, AI consistently delivers 14–40% improvements in speed and quality across writing, coding, and analysis. However, these gains are concentrated "inside the frontier" — tasks where AI is genuinely capable. Outside that boundary, AI use can *reduce* performance by up to 19 percentage points (Dell'Acqua et al., Harvard/BCG, 2023).

### 2. What separates high performers is not frequency — it's how they use AI
Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index identifies a "Frontier Professional" cohort (top 16% of users). These users don't just use AI more often; they redesign workflows around it, exercise quality control on output, and apply critical thinking. 80% report producing work that was previously impossible for them.

### 3. Self-reported AI skill is unreliable
A 2025 Pluralsight survey found 92% of professionals feel confident in their AI skills, yet organizations continue to struggle with AI project failures. Research shows a "reverse Dunning-Kruger" effect: AI-literate users can be *more* overconfident than novices due to cognitive offloading. Scenario-based assessment is essential.

### 4. Five dimensions capture AI adoption maturity
Synthesizing the OECD/EC AILit framework, UNESCO competency frameworks, the A-Factor psychometric research, and workplace evidence, we propose five dimensions: (1) AI Understanding & Literacy, (2) Practical Application & Tool Use, (3) Quality Control & Critical Evaluation, (4) Workflow Integration & Strategy, (5) Responsible & Ethical Use.

### 5. No defensible global benchmark exists yet
No public dataset supports a valid "percentile vs. everyone." Microsoft's Frontier Professional designation (16%) is the closest, but it's a single survey. We recommend fixed skill-level bands first, then internal team percentiles as data accumulates.

### 6. Organizational factors matter twice as much as individual behavior
Microsoft's 2026 data show that culture, manager support, and talent practices account for 67% of AI impact, versus 32% for individual mindset. Any score must acknowledge this context.

### 7. The "joy paradox" is real
BCG's 2026 data show AI improves job satisfaction for two-thirds of regular users, but increases cognitive load for 41%. Effective AI use means managing AI output, not just generating it.

### 8. Validated psychometric instruments now exist
The A-Factor framework (Li et al., 2025, N=517) identifies a dominant latent factor explaining 44% of variance in AI interaction tasks, measured across four sub-dimensions. Several validated scales (AILQ-C, GAIL-PT) now have Cronbach's alpha of 0.89–0.94.

### 9. Evidence-based improvement actions are specific and actionable
Structured training delivers ~$3.70 return per dollar. Peer-led learning, hands-on practice with real tasks, and role-specific curricula are far more effective than passive e-learning. The four-layer approach (Literacy → Tools → Risk → Advanced Application) is well-supported.

### 10. 10 questions is the right length for this context
Psychometric literature shows 10–12 items provide an optimal balance of reliability and completion rate for non-clinical workplace assessments. Completion rates drop significantly beyond 12 items, and diminishing reliability returns set in around 20 items. We use 10 questions (2 per dimension) with a mix of self-report and scenario-based items.

## Top Recommendations

1. **Use 5 dimensions, 10 questions, fixed-level scoring** (not percentiles until internal data accumulates).
2. **Mix question types**: at least 40% scenario-based to counter self-report bias.
3. **Anchor recommendations in evidence**: improvement suggestions should follow the four-layer training model.
4. **Acknowledge organizational context**: the score measures individual readiness, not organizational maturity.
5. **Plan for validation**: pilot with 50+ users, measure internal consistency, and refine before any high-stakes use.
