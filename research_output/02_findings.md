# Research Findings

## a. How People Actually Use AI at Work — and What Separates High Performers

### Usage Patterns
As of mid-2026, AI use at work is mainstream: **52% of U.S. employees** use AI in their roles (Gallup, 2026), with 15% using it daily. Organizational adoption has reached **88%** globally (Stanford HAI, 2026). The most common uses are writing, research, problem-solving, and coding.

However, usage is heavily concentrated in high-skill, analytical tasks. Anthropic's analysis of 2 million real-world conversations found that AI tasks require an average of **14.4 years of education** to perform (Anthropic Economic Primitives, Jan 2026). Augmentation (52% of usage) slightly outpaces full task automation (45%).

### What Separates High Performers
Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index identifies **"Frontier Professionals"** — the top 16% of AI users globally. Their distinguishing behaviors are:

1. **Workflow redesign**: They don't just use AI for one-off tasks — they restructure their work around AI. 80% report producing work that was previously impossible for them.
2. **Quality control**: The #1 valued human skill is **quality control of AI output** (50% of respondents), followed by critical thinking (46%).
3. **Knowing the boundary**: The Harvard/BCG "jagged frontier" study (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023, N=758) showed that consultants who used AI for tasks within its capability zone were 25% faster and produced 40% higher quality work. But those who used AI *outside* that zone were 19 percentage points more likely to produce incorrect solutions.
4. **Managerial mindset**: High performers treat AI as a team member to direct and review, not a tool to blindly accept output from. They build repeatable prompt templates, test against edge cases, and iterate.
5. **Model awareness**: They choose different AI tools for different tasks and understand limitations.

### Measured Productivity Outcomes
- **Customer support**: +14% resolved issues per hour (multiple studies)
- **Software development**: +26% completed tasks (GitHub Copilot studies)
- **Writing tasks**: 25–40% faster with quality improvements (Harvard/BCG)
- **IT troubleshooting**: 87% faster (OpenAI Enterprise Report, N=9,000, 2025)
- **Marketing execution**: 85% faster (same source)

**Important caveat**: These are task-level gains. BCG's 2026 survey found that while 80% of employees report improved individual productivity, **most organizations have not captured this as bottom-line value**. McKinsey reports only ~1% of companies are at full AI maturity.

### Consultancy / Industry Assessments
| Tool | Scope | Validated? | Notes |
|------|-------|-----------|-------|
| AIBT AI Critical Thinking Assessment | Workplace simulation-based | **Partial** — performance scored | Uses scenarios, not self-report |

**Key finding**: Most frameworks are conceptual or targeted at education. No validated, widely-adopted workplace AI proficiency assessment exists at scale.

## c. Proposed Dimensions for the AI Adoption Score

Based on synthesis across the OECD/EC AILit framework (Engage, Create, Manage, Shape), UNESCO (Understand, Apply, Create), the A-Factor research (communication, creation, evaluation, collaboration), and workplace evidence (Microsoft, BCG, Anthropic), we propose **five dimensions**:

### Dimension 1: AI Understanding & Literacy (Weight: 15%)
**What it measures**: Foundational knowledge of what AI can and cannot do, how it works conceptually, and awareness of key limitations (hallucinations, bias, data boundaries).

**Evidence for inclusion**: The Harvard/BCG study directly showed that users who understood AI's boundary outperformed those who didn't. The OECD AILit framework places "Engage with AI" as its first domain. The EU AI Act mandates this level of literacy.

**Measurement method**: 2 questions — 1 self-report (frequency/breadth of knowledge), 1 scenario-based (identify a limitation in a real situation).

### Dimension 2: Practical Application & Tool Use (Weight: 25%)
**What it measures**: Ability to effectively use AI tools for work tasks — prompting skill, task selection, tool selection, and getting useful results.

**Evidence for inclusion**: The A-Factor research identifies "communication effectiveness" as a core dimension. Microsoft's Frontier Professional data shows workflow application as the key differentiator. Anthropic data shows augmentation is the dominant use pattern.

**Measurement method**: 2 questions — 1 self-report (frequency and diversity of use), 1 scenario-based (choose the best approach for a work task).

### Dimension 3: Quality Control & Critical Evaluation (Weight: 25%)
**What it measures**: Ability to review, verify, and improve AI output. Detecting errors, hallucinations, bias, and knowing when AI output is unreliable.

**Evidence for inclusion**: Microsoft 2026 data shows quality control is the #1 valued human skill (50%). The Harvard/BCG study showed that blind reliance on AI outside its frontier degrades performance by 19pp. BCG's "joy paradox" shows cognitive load increases with AI, driven by output management.

**Measurement method**: 2 questions — 1 scenario-based (spot the problem in AI output), 1 self-report (verification habits).

### Dimension 4: Workflow Integration & Strategy (Weight: 20%)
**What it measures**: Whether the person has moved beyond one-off use to systematically integrating AI into their work processes, redesigning workflows, and building repeatable approaches.

**Evidence for inclusion**: Microsoft's Frontier Professional definition centers on workflow redesign. BCG data shows that strategic clarity drives organizational AI value. The shift from "prompting" to "workflow design" is the key behavioral marker of 2026's high performers.

**Measurement method**: 2 questions — 1 self-report (describe integration level), 1 scenario-based (how would you approach a multi-step task).

### Dimension 5: Responsible & Ethical Use (Weight: 15%)
**What it measures**: Awareness of and adherence to privacy, data security, fairness, and ethical considerations when using AI at work.

**Evidence for inclusion**: The EU AI Act makes this a legal obligation. UNESCO places ethics as a core competency. Anthropic's data shows concerns about deskilling and labor market impact. BCG and Gallup data show "shadow AI" (unsanctioned use) is a growing risk.

**Measurement method**: 2 questions — 1 scenario-based (what would you do with sensitive data), 1 self-report (awareness of policies).

### Why These Weights?
- **Practical Application and Quality Control** get the highest weights (25% each) because the evidence consistently shows these are where the performance gap between average and high users is widest.
- **Workflow Integration** gets 20% because it's the frontier behavior that separates the top 16% but is less foundational.
- **Understanding and Ethics** each get 15% because they're necessary foundations but less directly predictive of effective AI use.

## d. Percentile and Benchmark Considerations

### Can we benchmark against "everyone" or "the best users in the world"?
**No.** Honestly and plainly: no public dataset supports this.

- **Microsoft's Frontier Professional data** is the closest: they report 16% of global users qualify. But this is based on their proprietary survey methodology, not a normalized psychometric instrument with published norms.
- **Anthropic's Economic Index** provides usage patterns but not individual competency scores.
- **No published norm tables** exist for any AI proficiency assessment comparable to, say, IQ or standardized test norms.

### Recommended Approach
1. **Phase 1 (launch)**: Use **fixed skill-level bands** based on score ranges. These are anchored to behavioral descriptions derived from the research, not to population comparisons.
   - 0–20: Starting Out
   - 21–40: Building Foundations
   - 41–60: Developing Proficiency
   - 61–80: Advanced Practitioner
   - 81–100: Leading

2. **Phase 2 (internal data)**: Once 50+ team members have taken the assessment, calculate **team percentiles**. This is defensible because the comparison group is known and consistent.

3. **Phase 3 (external data)**: If a published norm emerges (e.g., from PISA 2029 or a large-scale validated instrument), adopt it. Until then, do not fabricate one.

## e. Known Measurement Problems

### Self-Report Bias
The most critical issue. A 2025 Pluralsight survey found 92% of professionals feel confident in their AI skills despite widespread competence gaps. Research in *Computers in Human Behavior* (2025) documents a "reverse Dunning-Kruger" effect where AI-literate users show *greater* overconfidence due to cognitive offloading — they trust AI output without verification and attribute the quality to their own skill.

**Mitigation**: Mix self-report and scenario-based questions. We use 50% scenario-based questions in our instrument.

### Overconfidence
Related to self-report bias but distinct: users who generate fluent AI output may believe they've mastered the underlying domain. Because AI produces authoritative-sounding text, users mistake output quality for personal competence.

**Mitigation**: Scenario questions that require *evaluation* of AI output, not just generation.

### Gaming
If consequences are attached to the score (promotion, recognition), people will optimize for the test rather than genuine skill development.

**Mitigation**: Frame as a development tool, not a performance rating. Use non-obvious correct answers in scenarios.

### Frequency vs. Quality
Using AI every day does not mean using it well. Someone who uses AI for trivial tasks frequently may score higher on usage frequency than someone who uses it strategically for high-value tasks.

**Mitigation**: Weight *how* over *how much*. The scoring model values depth of integration and quality practices over raw frequency.

### Role and Industry Differences
A software engineer's ideal AI use differs fundamentally from a field researcher's. The "right" answers for scenario questions may vary by role.

**Mitigation**: Keep questions role-agnostic (about general AI interaction skills) rather than domain-specific. Acknowledge in the results that scores reflect general AI readiness, not role-specific optimization.

### Fairness Concerns
- **Access bias**: People with better AI tools or more supportive organizations will naturally score higher, regardless of personal aptitude.
- **Digital divide**: Differences in technology access, internet quality, and organizational investment create structural advantages.
- **Language bias**: AI tools perform differently across languages; most research is in English.

**Mitigation**: Include questions about understanding and judgment (which are access-independent). State clearly that the score measures readiness, which includes but is not limited to access.

### Mixed Evidence on AI Productivity
The evidence is genuinely contested at the organizational level:
- **For**: Task-level studies consistently show 14–40% gains (Harvard/BCG, OpenAI, GitHub).
- **Against/Nuanced**: McKinsey reports only ~1% of firms at AI maturity. Some studies show AI can increase workload rather than reduce it. The BIS (Bank for International Settlements, 2026) notes traditional productivity statistics may not capture AI gains. Entry-level workers in AI-exposed fields face reduced hiring (Anthropic, Mar 2026).
- **Our position**: Task-level gains are well-established. Organization-level gains depend on complementary investments. We do not claim AI universally improves productivity.

## f. Minimum Validation Plan

### Pilot Size
- **Minimum**: 50 respondents for initial reliability analysis (Cronbach's alpha, item-total correlations).
- **Recommended**: 100+ for exploratory factor analysis to confirm the 5-dimension structure.
- **Gold standard**: 200+ for confirmatory factor analysis.

### Reliability Checks
1. **Internal consistency**: Calculate Cronbach's alpha for each dimension and overall. Target: α ≥ 0.70 for each dimension.
2. **Item-total correlations**: Each item should correlate at r ≥ 0.30 with its dimension total. Remove or revise items below this threshold.
3. **Test-retest reliability** (if feasible): Re-administer to a subset after 2–4 weeks. Target: r ≥ 0.70.

### Validity Checks
1. **Content validity**: Achieved through derivation from established frameworks (OECD, UNESCO, A-Factor) and review by subject matter experts.
2. **Construct validity**: Factor analysis should confirm the 5-dimension structure.
3. **Criterion validity**: Correlate scores with observable indicators (e.g., frequency of AI tool use from IT logs, manager ratings of AI effectiveness, self-reported productivity gains). Even modest correlations (r = 0.20–0.40) would be meaningful.
4. **Discriminant validity**: Ensure the score doesn't simply measure general tech-savviness or job performance. Scores should discriminate between AI-specific skills and general digital literacy.

### Refinement Plan
1. **After pilot (50 users)**: Review item statistics, remove/revise weak items, adjust weights if factor analysis suggests different loadings.
2. **After 100 users**: Run exploratory factor analysis. Confirm or revise the 5-dimension model.
3. **After 200+ users**: Confirmatory factor analysis. Establish internal team norms.
4. **Ongoing**: Track score distributions over time to detect ceiling effects (too many people scoring high) or floor effects. Adjust difficulty as AI skills evolve.

## g. What Actually Helps People Use AI More Effectively

The evidence points to specific, actionable interventions:

### 1. Hands-On Practice with Real Tasks (Strongest Evidence)
Passive training (videos, slide decks) is significantly less effective than interactive practice with actual work tasks. Organizations that embed AI practice into daily workflows see measurably higher adoption and skill development.

**Source**: Multiple 2025–2026 training effectiveness studies; BCG and McKinsey organizational surveys.

### 2. The Four-Layer Training Approach
The most robust training programs follow a structured progression:
1. **Literacy**: What AI is, how it works conceptually, and key limitations.
2. **Tools**: Hands-on proficiency with specific platforms the organization uses.
3. **Risk Management**: Privacy, data handling, bias detection, hallucination identification.
4. **Advanced Application**: Integrating AI into complex decisions and cross-functional workflows.

**Source**: Synthesized from multiple training effectiveness studies, 2025–2026.

### 3. Peer-Led Learning
Weekly peer huddles or "AI learning days" scale adoption more effectively than top-down mandates. People learn best from colleagues who share their context and challenges.

**Source**: Organizational adoption studies, BCG 2026, multiple workplace learning reports.

### 4. Manager Support and Strategic Clarity
Gallup data shows manager support is the #1 factor in successful AI adoption. When managers actively support AI use, employees are significantly more likely to use tools frequently and report meaningful transformation. Companies with optimistic, growth-oriented AI narratives see 30% higher adoption rates.

**Source**: Gallup 2025–2026 tracker; McKinsey 2026 survey.

### 5. Quality Control as a Habit
Training people to systematically verify AI output — check facts, review reasoning, test edge cases — is the single highest-leverage skill intervention. Microsoft's data shows this is the #1 valued human skill in AI-augmented work.

**Source**: Microsoft WTI 2026; Harvard/BCG "jagged frontier" study.

### 6. Role-Specific Curricula
Generic "AI 101" training is less effective than training tailored to how specific roles can use AI. A marketing manager needs different AI skills than a data analyst.

**Source**: Training effectiveness literature, 2025–2026.

### 7. Start Small, Build Confidence
The most effective adoption paths start with low-risk, high-reward tasks (email drafting, meeting summaries, research compilation) before moving to complex applications. This builds confidence and reduces the fear of AI replacing jobs.

**Source**: Behavioral adoption research; Gallup workplace sentiment data.
